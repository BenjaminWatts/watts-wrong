# Chapter 14: CfD vs Marginal Pricing - Market Design Disasters

There is an urban myth that all British electricity is expensive because of the role of gas fired power.

## How Marginal Pricing Works in a Gas Market

To understand the problems with Britain's electricity market design, we first need to understand how prices were set in the old world of fossil fuel generation. The principle is called marginal pricing: at any given moment, the price of electricity is set by the most expensive generator needed to meet demand.

Picture a fleet of gas power stations, each with different characteristics. The newest combined-cycle gas turbines (CCGTs) might convert 60% of their fuel into electricity. Older plants from the 1990s might manage only 45%. The oldest peaking plants - often open-cycle gas turbines designed for quick starts rather than efficiency - might struggle to reach 35%. When gas costs the same for everyone, these efficiency differences translate directly into different costs per megawatt-hour of electricity produced.

In a typical day, as demand rises from its overnight low to the morning peak, the grid operator calls on progressively more expensive generators. First, the most efficient CCGTs run flat out. Then older, less efficient plants are switched on. Finally, if demand is high enough, the expensive peaking plants fire up. Each time a less efficient plant is needed, the wholesale price hops up to reflect its higher costs - and every other generator running at that moment receives that same higher price.

But generators don't just bid their raw fuel costs. They need to recover their fixed costs: the capital to build the plant, grid connection charges, and ongoing maintenance. They also need to cover the hidden costs of flexibility - the fuel burned and equipment wear from starting up and shutting down, sometimes multiple times per day. These costs get baked into their bids, adding a margin above pure fuel costs.

This creates a peculiar pattern in electricity prices. In times of comfortable surplus, when only the most efficient plants are needed, competition keeps margins thin. But as the system tightens - whether from high demand, plant outages, or failures of interconnectors bringing power from abroad - those margins can expand dramatically. The last generator needed to keep the lights on has considerable pricing power.

For peaking plants, this dynamic defined their entire business model. A gas peaker might sit idle for 350 days a year, burning no fuel and earning nothing. But on those handful of bitterly cold winter days when demand surged and other plants broke down, they could name almost any price. A significant proportion of their annual profits came from perhaps a dozen tight days on the grid. The rest of their income came from capacity payments - essentially being paid to exist and remain available for emergencies.

This system had a certain brutal logic to it. Prices signalled scarcity. Investment followed price signals. Plants that were rarely needed could still justify their existence through occasional price spikes and standby payments. The market, in theory, would deliver just enough capacity to keep the lights on.

## Pumped Storage: Smoothing Without Breaking

Britain's geography is not blessed with many mountains, but the few we have enabled a clever solution to the problem of demand spikes: pumped storage hydroelectricity. Plants like Dinorwig in Snowdonia, Ffestiniog nearby, and Cruachan in the Scottish Highlands work on a simple principle. When electricity is cheap - typically overnight when demand is low and efficient baseload plants are ticking over - they use that power to pump water uphill into a high reservoir. When prices spike during peak demand, they release that water back down through turbines, generating electricity in seconds.

This arbitrage - buying low, selling high - gave pumped storage a natural business model within the marginal pricing system. Post privatistion, the operators made money from the price spread between off-peak and peak. But in doing so, they also moderated the very spikes they profited from. By injecting supply precisely when it was most valuable, they shaved the peaks and filled the troughs, flattening the price curve without fundamentally disrupting how the market worked. As far as I'm aware, the existing plants have not required substantive subsidy or support at any point. 

The most spectacular demonstrations of this came during television pickups. When a popular programme ended - the final whistle of a World Cup match, the closing credits of a royal wedding, or the climax of a soap opera - millions of Britons would simultaneously switch on their kettles. Demand could surge by nearly 3 gigawatts in the space of minutes. Dinorwig, capable of going from standstill to 1.8 gigawatts in under 16 seconds, became the unsung hero of the nation's tea-drinking habits, which it's worth noting are noteworthy by international standards (most countries aren't quite as obsessed boiling 2-3KW kettles to make tea!) Either way. the grid kept the lights on, the kettles boiled, and the market still made sense.

Crucially, pumped storage remained a niche player - a useful tool for managing peaks rather than a dominant force reshaping the market. Britain had perhaps 3 gigawatts of pumped storage capacity, enough to help with the sharpest spikes but not enough to change the fundamental economics. Gas plants still set the marginal price most of the time. The merit order still worked. Price signals still guided investment. It was optimisation within the system, not a challenge to it.

There is another important detail: all of Britain's pumped storage capacity was built under public ownership before privatisation. Ffestiniog was completed by the Central Electricity Generating Board in 1963, Cruachan by the North of Scotland Hydro-Electric Board in 1965, Foyers in 1974, and Dinorwig - the largest civil engineering contract the UK government had ever awarded at the time - in 1984. When the electricity industry was privatised in 1990, these assets were simply transferred to the new private owners. The private sector inherited pumped storage; it did not finance or build it.

This distinction matters because no new pumped storage has been built in Britain since privatisation - a gap of over 40 years. The economics of these projects, with their massive upfront capital costs and revenues dependent on volatile price spreads, have proven incomprehensible to private finance without government support. The largest proposed scheme, Coire Glas in the Scottish Highlands, would add 1.5 gigawatts and double Britain's long-duration storage capacity. But SSE Renewables will only build it with explicit revenue stabilisation from Ofgem's new Long Duration Energy Storage (LDES) cap and floor mechanism - essentially a guarantee that revenues will not fall below a minimum level, in exchange for capping the upside. Even for assets that worked elegantly within the old market, private capital now demands protection from that market's volatility.

## When the Wind Blows: Zero Marginal Cost Meets Marginal Pricing

Wind power broke the logic of the electricity market in a way that pumped storage never could. The reason is simple: wind has no fuel cost. Once a turbine is built, the marginal cost of generating the next megawatt-hour is essentially zero. The same is true, to a lesser extent, of solar - though in Britain's grey climate, wind is the dominant force.

In a marginal pricing system, generators are dispatched in order of their costs, cheapest first. Wind, with its zero marginal cost, jumps straight to the front of the queue. When the wind blows strongly, turbines generate flat out and push gas plants off the system entirely. Wholesale prices collapse. On particularly windy days with low demand, prices can even turn negative - generators effectively paying to keep running rather than face the costs of switching off.

But negative prices are not simply about avoiding shutdown costs. They reveal something deeper about how renewables subsidies interact with wholesale markets. Wind farms receiving support through the old Renewables Obligation scheme, or through Contracts for Difference, earn their subsidies per megawatt-hour generated. If they are curtailed - told to stop generating because the grid cannot absorb their output - they lose not just the wholesale price but the subsidy payment too. For a wind farm on a generous legacy contract, that subsidy can be worth far more than the wholesale price itself. It makes economic sense for them to bid negative, effectively paying to generate, as long as the negative price they accept is smaller than the subsidy they would forfeit by switching off. The result is that on very windy, low-demand days, the market clears at prices that make no sense in isolation - but make perfect sense when you understand the subsidy arithmetic.

Consider how strange this market has become. Gas plants bid positive prices because they have fuel costs - every megawatt-hour they generate costs them money in gas burned. If they are told not to run, they save that fuel cost; being curtailed is a relief. Wind farms bid negative prices because they have subsidies to protect - every megawatt-hour they fail to generate costs them money in support payments foregone. If they are told not to run, they lose income; being curtailed is a penalty. The same market now contains generators who pay to avoid running and generators who pay to keep running. The marginal pricing system was designed for a world where all generators had positive costs and preferred not to run unless paid. It was never designed for this.

This creates a real distortion in how the electricity system operates. In a well-functioning market, wind farms would bid at or near zero - their true marginal cost - and the grid would dispatch generation efficiently based on actual economics. Instead, negative bidding by subsidised wind forces gas plants off the system earlier than pure economics would dictate. The gas plants that remain online must ramp down further, or switch off entirely, only to ramp back up hours later when the wind eases.

Gas turbines are not designed for this kind of treatment. Most of Britain's combined-cycle gas turbines were engineered and financed on the assumption they would run for long stretches - baseload or mid-merit operation, not constant cycling. They have minimum stable generation levels below which they cannot safely operate. Each startup-shutdown cycle imposes real costs: fuel burned during warmup, thermal stress on components, and maintenance bills that accumulate with every cycle.

Worse still, gas plants face what engineers call failure-to-return risk. A turbine that shuts down overnight and goes cold may not restart cleanly in the morning. Components can fail during the thermal stress of warming up. If a plant cannot return when the wind drops and prices spike, it misses precisely the hours that justify its existence. This creates a perverse incentive: some gas plants keep running at low or even negative prices overnight, not because it makes economic sense in that moment, but because the costs and risks of shutting down and restarting outweigh the losses from generating when not needed. The market was not designed for generators who would rather lose money than switch off.

The result is that gas plants sometimes reinforce the very price volatility that renewables create. By accepting losses during windy nights and sunny midday periods to stay warm and reduce restart risk, they add supply precisely when supply is already abundant, pushing prices down further still. It is the opposite of what a rational market should produce.

The solution, in principle, is more flexibility elsewhere in the system. Demand-side response - paying industrial users to reduce consumption when supply is tight, or shifting electric vehicle charging to times of surplus - could absorb some of the variability. Battery storage, now coming online at scale, can soak up cheap midday solar and discharge it during evening peaks. With enough flexibility on the demand side and from storage, whatever gas generation remains could operate in a more stable, predictable way.

Another option is to replace inflexible combined-cycle plants with more nimble alternatives: open-cycle gas turbines or reciprocating engines that can start and stop quickly without the same thermal stress. These technologies are cheaper to build but less efficient and higher in emissions per unit of electricity. If they are used sparingly - genuine peaking capacity for emergencies rather than daily cycling - that might be a tradeoff worth accepting. The capital saved could be invested in additional renewable capacity or energy efficiency measures that reduce the need for gas generation in the first place.

But even this path faces an unexpected obstacle. The artificial intelligence boom has created extraordinary demand for power generation equipment of all kinds. Gas turbines, reciprocating engines, even diesel generators are in high demand worldwide, with lead times stretching out as data centre developers scramble to secure supply. Some hyperscalers have reportedly explored repurposing aircraft jet engines - which are, after all, similar gas turbines to those found in power stations - for at least temporary power generation. The market for flexibility is global, and Britain is competing for equipment with some of the world's most deep-pocketed buyers.

Forcing gas plants to dance to the tune of subsidy-driven wind bids rather than genuine supply and demand imposes costs on the system that show up nowhere in the wholesale price.

The real impact shows up not in the wholesale market but in the balancing mechanism - the system the grid operator uses to fine-tune supply and demand in real time. As wind has grown, gas plants have seen their running hours collapse to historic lows. By 2024, even during the most profitable hours, less than half of Britain's combined-cycle gas turbine capacity was online. Yet paradoxically, gas still dominates the balancing mechanism precisely because it can ramp up and down quickly. The same plants that are being squeezed out of the main market are being paid handsomely to provide flexibility services - turning up when wind drops, turning down when it surges.

The costs are substantial and growing. Balancing costs reached £830 million in 2024, with projections suggesting they could rise towards £5 billion by 2030 as more wind comes online and the grid struggles to cope. Contrary to popular perception, the majority of these costs - around three-quarters - go to gas generators being paid to turn up or provide backup, not to wind farms being paid to turn off. The system has evolved into something strange: we pay wind farms subsidies to generate, then pay them again when we need them to stop, while simultaneously paying gas plants to stand ready and ramp on demand. The wholesale market tells one story; the balancing mechanism tells another entirely.

This sounds like good news for consumers. And in those windy moments, it is. But the problem is what happens when the wind drops. Britain's electricity demand doesn't follow the weather. People still want to boil kettles, run factories, and heat homes regardless of whether it's blowing a gale or dead calm. When wind output falls, gas plants must fire up to fill the gap. And when gas plants are needed, gas sets the marginal price - for all the electricity on the system, including whatever wind is still generating.

The result is a strange hybrid. On a windy afternoon, wholesale prices might be £30 per megawatt-hour or less. By evening, as wind drops and demand peaks, the same market might clear at £150. The next day, with different weather, the pattern could reverse entirely. Prices no longer follow the predictable rhythm of human activity - high in the morning, low overnight, peaking on cold winter evenings. They follow the weather, which follows its own logic entirely.

This volatility creates a deeper problem: the more wind capacity Britain builds, the lower wholesale prices fall during windy periods. Economists call this the cannibalisation effect. Wind farms are most productive precisely when every other wind farm is also most productive, which is precisely when their output is least valuable. A technology that makes electricity cheaper in aggregate can simultaneously make its own revenues collapse.

For investors trying to finance a wind farm with a 25-year lifespan, this is a nightmare. The project's revenues depend on wholesale prices that swing wildly from hour to hour and are likely to decline structurally over time as more wind is built. Banks, unsurprisingly, are reluctant to lend against such uncertain cashflows. The very success of wind power in reducing wholesale prices threatened to make new wind projects unfinanceable.

Solar faces the same fundamental problem, though in Britain its role is smaller. Solar output is at least predictable in its timing - it generates during daylight hours, with a peak around midday. But this predictability creates its own cannibalisation: every solar panel in the country produces power at the same time, depressing midday prices. And of course, solar produces nothing during the winter evening peaks when electricity is most valuable and most expensive.

The market that worked for gas - where price signals guided investment and revenues rewarded scarcity - was breaking down. Something had to give.

## The Private Solution: Power Purchase Agreements

The first response to this financing problem came from the private sector. Power Purchase Agreements - PPAs - are bilateral contracts negotiated directly between a generator and a buyer, bypassing the wholesale market entirely. A wind farm developer might sign a 15-year deal with a large energy supplier or an industrial customer, agreeing to sell their output at a fixed price, perhaps indexed to inflation.

For the generator, a PPA provides the revenue certainty that banks demand before they will lend. Instead of facing the chaos of hourly wholesale prices, the wind farm knows exactly what it will earn per megawatt-hour for the life of the contract. For the buyer - whether an energy supplier hedging its costs or a corporation wanting to claim green credentials - the PPA offers price stability and a direct link to renewable generation.

PPAs have grown substantially, particularly among large technology companies and industrial consumers seeking to lock in energy costs and demonstrate their sustainability commitments. But they have limits. Negotiating bespoke contracts is expensive and time-consuming. Only the largest, most creditworthy buyers can offer the long-term commitments that developers need. And PPAs do nothing to help smaller developers or less bankable projects. For the broader challenge of scaling up renewable capacity, something more systematic was needed.

## The Government Solution: Contracts for Difference

The government's answer was Contracts for Difference - CfDs. The principle is elegant: rather than trying to fix the wholesale market, work around it. A CfD is essentially an insurance policy that guarantees a renewable generator a fixed "strike price" for their electricity, regardless of what the wholesale market does.

When wholesale prices fall below the strike price, the generator receives a top-up payment from a fund levied on consumer bills. When wholesale prices rise above the strike price, the generator pays the difference back. From the developer's perspective, revenue is stabilised - they know what they will earn regardless of market volatility. From the consumer's perspective, the cost of renewable electricity is also stabilised - they pay the strike price either way, shielded from both the highs and lows of the wholesale market.

The CfD scheme has been the engine of Britain's renewable buildout. Around 9 gigawatts of capacity has already been delivered through CfDs and their predecessor contracts, with a further 26 gigawatts contracted to become operational by 2030. The government's Clean Power 2030 plan envisions gas generation falling to just 5% of the total, with CfD-backed renewables forming the backbone of a 95% clean power system.

As CfDs come to dominate the generation mix, a profound shift occurs in what the wholesale market actually means. The wholesale price will still exist, and it will still matter for balancing the grid in real time. But it will become increasingly irrelevant for determining what consumers actually pay. The more electricity that flows under CfD contracts, the more consumer prices reflect strike prices negotiated years in advance rather than the daily gyrations of gas and wind. The wholesale market becomes a technical mechanism for grid operation rather than the place where electricity's value is truly determined.

## The Disaster: A Market That No Longer Makes Sense

Step back and consider what Britain has built. We have a wholesale electricity market designed in the 1990s for a world of competing gas plants, where marginal pricing sent efficient signals about scarcity and guided rational investment. That market still exists. Generators still bid, prices still clear every half hour, and the marginal plant still sets the price for everyone.

But layered on top of that market, we now have Contracts for Difference that guarantee fixed revenues regardless of wholesale prices. We have a Balancing Mechanism that pays generators to adjust output in real time, with costs approaching a billion pounds a year and rising fast. We have a Capacity Market that pays gas plants simply to exist and remain available. We have constraint payments that compensate both wind farms and gas plants when the grid cannot physically accommodate their output. We have the remnants of the Renewables Obligation, with legacy contracts that will run for years to come.

Each of these mechanisms was added for sensible reasons. CfDs solved the financing problem for renewables. The Balancing Mechanism keeps the lights on when forecasts prove wrong. The Capacity Market ensures backup exists for windless winter evenings. Constraint payments prevent generators from bearing losses for grid inadequacy beyond their control. Individually, each patch addressed a real problem.

Collectively, they amount to a system that has lost any coherent logic. The wholesale market price - supposedly the signal that coordinates the entire system - has become almost meaningless. It no longer guides investment, because developers look to CfD strike prices instead. It no longer ensures efficient dispatch, because subsidised wind farms bid negative to protect their support payments. It no longer tells consumers what electricity actually costs, because their bills reflect levies, network charges, and policy costs that dwarf the wholesale component. The market exists, but it no longer does what markets are supposed to do.

The result is that consumers pay multiple times for the same electricity system. They pay wholesale prices when they buy power. They pay the CfD levy that funds top-up payments to renewable generators. They pay network charges that cover the cost of grid reinforcement. They pay for the Capacity Market that keeps gas plants on standby. They pay balancing costs when the system needs real-time adjustment. They pay constraint costs when Scottish wind cannot reach English demand. Each payment has its own logic, but no one designed them to work together, and no one can easily explain what the total is buying.

Meanwhile, the price signals that do exist point in contradictory directions. Wholesale prices tell wind farms their output is worthless or worse on windy afternoons, while CfD payments tell them to generate anyway. Wholesale prices tell gas plants they are uneconomic most of the time, while capacity payments tell them to stay open regardless. Wholesale prices swing wildly with the weather, while the costs that actually matter to consumers barely move at all. The market speaks, but nobody is listening - because listening to it would lead to decisions that make no sense.

This is the market design disaster at the heart of Britain's electricity system. Not that the wrong choices were made, necessarily, but that choices were made piecemeal, each solving yesterday's problem while creating tomorrow's. We kept a marginal pricing market that made sense for gas, bolted on subsidies for renewables that distort that market's signals, added mechanisms to manage the chaos those distortions create, and ended up with a system so complex that almost no one understands how it actually works - or what it would take to make it work better.

The tragedy is one of missed potential. The capital costs of wind turbines and solar panels have fallen dramatically over the past decade, and Britain sits on some of the best wind resources in Europe. But as Michael Liebreich of Bloomberg New Energy Finance has observed, clean energy "almost always involves higher capital costs followed by lower fuel and maintenance costs" - which means it is "only cheap if you have access to cheap capital." For renewables, financing is everything.

The timing since 2022 could hardly have been worse. Interest rates rose sharply just as gas prices fell back from their crisis peaks to more normal levels - the worst possible combination for renewable economics. Higher borrowing costs pushed up the financing burden on capital-intensive projects, while lower wholesale prices reduced the revenues those projects could expect. At the same time, inflation remained stubbornly persistent in labour costs and parts of the industrial supply chain, squeezing project economics from both sides. Recent CfD strike prices have risen even as the underlying technology continued to improve.

A market designed around the economics of renewables, rather than one designed for gas with renewables bolted on, might have unlocked cheaper financing, reduced risk premiums, and let more of those technology gains flow through to consumers. Instead, grid constraints waste billions paying generators not to generate. Balancing costs spiral as the system struggles to cope with variability it was never designed to handle. We have built a world-class renewable resource on top of a market structure that cannot make the most of it. That is the disaster.
